Key findings

  • Rapid growth, fragile firmness. Reported installed capacity roughly doubled from 593–699 MW for 2017 (EDG and World Bank figures differ) [59, 74] to 1,305.6 MW in 2025, and peak load served reached 961 MW [79]. The World Bank describes only about 1,000 MW as 'reliably available', a term it does not define [64]; no seasonal dependable capacity is documented for any hydro plant.
  • Hydrology is the system's swing factor. Natural Konkouré flows at Kaléta range from about 18 m³/s in April to 1,250 m³/s in August [23]. The 2024 drought cut hydro output by more than 10% [64], HFO burn on the grid rose from about 65,000 t (2023) to 118,762 t (2024) [78], and EDG reported 129,797 hours of load shedding for production deficit [79]. In 2025, a wet year, EDG's summary table shows 903 GWh of exports (unit not stated in the row) [79].
  • The grid into Conakry is a constraint in its own right. Conakry substation transformers ran at about 80–103% loading in EDG's latest published table, whose year (2022 or 2023) is ambiguous [82]. The Guinea–Mali line is about 85% complete on its World Bank-financed works but delayed [66], and Linsan–Fomi has been financed since 2021 with no works start evidenced [18].
  • The utility is not financially sustainable. The average tariff is about US¢10/kWh against an estimated cost of service of about US¢27/kWh [64]. Subsidies averaged 55% of revenue in 2019–2023 [64], payables to independent producers reached GNF 6,500 bn in 2023 [64], and EDG reported a GNF 430 bn loss for 2025 (labelled 'audited' by EDG; no audit opinion located) [79].
  • Demand is rising fast, but mining stays captive. Mining requires about 558 MW, mostly from captive generation [64]; refinery conventions provide for their own power plants [43]. The national plan's 3,894 MW for 2030 comprises 1,635 MW domestic demand, 1,442 MW mining demand and 817 MW of interconnection exports, loads that are not contracted with the grid [22].
  • The pipeline is long, commitments are short. About 379 MW of identified new generation with a stated capacity is at differing stages, and none of it is commissioned. Amaria (300 MW) is under construction and delayed [22, 30, 33]. Kankan (40 MW), three EBID-financed micro-hydro plants (27.6 MW) and Tinkisso 2 (11 MW) have signed financing but no evidenced construction start [11, 12, 13, 32, 79, 80]. Only Amaria has a published online year (2027) [22]. A further ~6.4 GW of hydro, thermal and solar (stated nameplate; may include duplicate listings) is at active-development, announced or proposed stage.
  • Financing needs far exceed commitments. The Mission 300 compact sets a 2025–2030 need of US$11.47 bn, of which US$8.90 bn remains to be raised [22]. Its US$2.56 bn 'already mobilised or in progress' [22] mixes signed loans with items that have no signed agreement, so it is not treated as committed financing. No World Bank Group energy guarantee is in use in Guinea [72].
  • Resources are large but unequally evidenced. Hydro technical potential is 6,233 MW according to the national hydropower atlas [35]. Modelled solar-resource values are withheld from this edition pending licence confirmation. Modelled onshore wind is weak (3.8 m/s mean at 100 m) with no measurements [10]. No source demonstrates commercially viable potential for any technology.

Key questions

1. What is Guinea's present electricity situation, and what are the principal constraints?

A hydro-dominated system of 1,305.6 MW installed capacity in 2025 [79], about two-thirds owned by independent producers selling to EDG as single buyer [22]. It served a record 961 MW peak and supplied 5,551 GWh in 2025 [79]. The principal constraints are dry-season firm supply (no documented dependable capacity; large load shedding in the 2024 drought [79]), a congested Conakry transmission and substation network, and a utility selling at about US¢10/kWh against an estimated cost of service of about US¢27/kWh [64].

2. Which parts of the system are binding: generation, dependable capacity, hydrology, transmission, distribution, finances or reliability?

All are binding, in different seasons and places. Annual energy was sufficient in 2025, a wet year with exports. Dry-season firm capacity was not: in 2024 EDG reported 129,797 hours of load shedding for production deficit [79], and its two annual reports give 793.8 GWh and 542.8 GWh of energy not distributed for that year [78, 79]. Grid constraints persist into Conakry. Losses are reported at about 30% for 2024 (theft, non-billing and technical, World Bank) and at 30% (technical and commercial) by EDG [22, 64], while a World Bank project document cites commercial losses of 40% in 2025 [64]. The measurement bases are not documented and the figures are not directly comparable. The evidence does not allow a quantified share for each factor; a daily load curve and seasonal dependable-capacity data are not published.

3. What demand growth is evidenced for 2027–2030, particularly from mining and industrialisation?

Peak load served nearly tripled from 334 MW (2017) to 961 MW (2025) [74, 79]. The national plan projects 3,894 MW by 2030, including 1,442 MW of mining demand and 817 MW of interconnection exports [22]; these loads are not contracted with EDG. Mining today requires about 558 MW, mostly captive [64]. Grid demand growth is therefore strong but uncertain in scale, and industrial loads may stay off-grid without new supply and access arrangements.

4. Which interventions have the strongest evidence-based rationale?

In order of evidential strength: (i) the already-financed transmission reinforcements (Manéah–Linsan, Linsan–Fomi, Guinea–Mali); (ii) completing Amaria and documenting the Konkouré cascade's dependable output and operating rules; (iii) utility recovery (metering, loss reduction, collections, an arrears plan for independent producers); (iv) firm evening and dry-season capacity, procured technology-open; (v) a frequency-containment battery, which a WAPP/GIZ study sizes at 13.2 MW for one hour for Guinea's regional reserve share [55] (a study recommendation for one grid service, not an adopted requirement and not a solution to dry-season shortfalls); (vi) grid-connected solar preceded by a grid-integration study; (vii) off-grid access through the GNEAP-2 programme and the national rural electrification plan [22, 64].

5. What are the major risks to project delivery, financing and energy security?

Execution after financing (Linsan–Fomi financed in 2021 with no works evidenced [18]; several co-financed access lots at 0% site progress at end-2025 [79]); hydrological and climate variability (hydro output in 2050 from −30% to +25% [71]); dependence on one river and on imported heavy fuel oil; EDG's finances and arrears; high country risk (OECD export-credit class 7 [39]); undocumented currency terms of power purchase agreements; and opaque terms for the largest loans.

6. What should be prioritised immediately, over the next three years, and through 2035?

See the Priorities tab. In short: document dependable capacity and cascade rules, start the delayed financed works, adopt tariff and arrears plans and procure firm capacity now; complete the grid backbone, commission Amaria and procure solar with storage on a hosting-capacity basis by 2029; and to 2035 select the next large hydro on dependable output and complete the sector framework.

7. What can government, utilities, private investors and development partners each contribute?

Government: legal framework, tariff path, payment discipline, land and permits, transparent procurement. EDG: system operation, metering, loss reduction and cascade dispatch. The regulator (AREE): tariffs, grid code, licensing. Development partners: concessional finance, guarantees (none yet used in Guinea's energy sector [72]), project preparation and technical assistance. Private investors: generation and storage, EPC and O&M, mini-grid concessions and supply to mining loads, once payment security and procurement frameworks exist.

8. Which projects or intervention categories warrant further feasibility work and due diligence?

The Konkouré cascade's dependable output; Koukoutamba, Fomi, Kogbédou-Frankonédou and Grand Kinkon (dependable output, transboundary rules, resettlement, financing); the Kamissaya/Kindia 50 MWc solar package (scope and financing reported inconsistently); the La Côtière 225 kV line; announced LNG-to-power projects; grid or shared supply to mining and refinery loads; storage beyond frequency reserve; wind (no measurements); and the currency and payment security of existing power purchase agreements.

National way forward

Strategic thesis. Guinea should establish dependable, deliverable and financially sustainable electricity supply before committing to a predetermined generation mix.
This is a sequencing recommendation drawn from the evidence, not a completed least-cost optimisation. The least-cost generation mix, solar and storage volumes and a project ranking cannot be determined responsibly until plant-level seasonal dependable capacity, an hourly load curve, quantified grid hosting and evacuation limits, and payment-security arrangements exist; none is published today.

Six findings that shape the way forward

Transmission execution is binding

Linsan–Fomi was financed in 2021 with no works start evidenced [18]; Manéah–Linsan is 41% disbursed but at 8% site progress [79]; Guinea–Mali is about 85% complete on its World Bank-financed works but delayed [66]; Conakry substation transformers ran at ≈80–103% loading in EDG's latest published table, whose year (2022 or 2023) is ambiguous [82].

EVIDENCE

La Côtière: stated purpose and inferred effect

Stated purpose in the Mission 300 compact: evacuating Kaléta–Souapiti output and supplying mining companies, with US$771 m still to mobilise [22]; Lot 1 is only 'under consideration' by UK Export Finance [50]. Research-team inference: until it is built, evacuation may limit how much existing hydro output can reach new loads; this has not been modelled.

EVIDENCE + INFERENCE

Solar scale-up: grid integration not yet quantified

Modelled solar-resource values are withheld from this edition pending licence confirmation. Grid hosting and evacuation limits are not quantified in the available evidence. The compact itself calls for correcting evacuation and stability constraints before absorbing large volumes of intermittent energy [22]. Hosting is one constraint among several: land (largely customary tenure) [47], payment security, PPA terms and the absence of a competitive procurement framework also bind.

EVIDENCE

Storage services differ; seasonal deficits need firm energy

Batteries can provide frequency response, short-duration power support and daily evening-peak shifting. Multi-day or multi-week deficits, such as the 2024 drought (hydro output down more than 10% [64]; 129,797 hours of load shedding for production deficit [79]; 543–794 GWh not distributed in two EDG figures [78, 79]), need firm energy, imports or reservoir management. The 13.2 MW, 1-hour battery is a WAPP/GIZ study recommendation for frequency containment, not an adopted requirement [55]. Illustrative scale only: one full discharge (≈13.2 MWh) is about 1/41,000 of the lower 2024 unserved-energy figure (542,767.5 MWh); the quantities measure different things and this is not a sizing calculation.

ESTIMATE

A few delays outweigh the pipeline size

Within the ≈379 MW subset under construction or with signed financing, Amaria is 300 MW, i.e. 79% (300 ÷ 378.6); this is a share of that subset, not of the national pipeline. Amaria is delayed at 61% overall execution [33]. About 6.4 GW of further stated nameplate is listed at active-development, announced or proposed stage (excluding a regional OMVG plant located in Senegal): a gross aggregation that may include duplicates, not expected, commissioned, dependable or available generation.

ESTIMATE

A first private renewable deal could set the precedent

No grid-connected solar plant operates [22]; no World Bank Group energy guarantee is in use [72]; PPA currency terms are undocumented. A first transparently contracted, bankable private renewable transaction could establish contract and risk-allocation templates for later investment.

Five national priorities, in dependency order

P1

Deliver financed transmission

Complete Linsan–Fomi, Manéah–Linsan and Guinea–Mali; reinforce Conakry substations; close financing for La Côtière.

Depends on: Financing largely exists
Decision gate: Works schedules published; contracts awarded; La Côtière financing decided

P2

Establish a firm-capacity baseline

Measure plant-level seasonal dependable capacity, adopt cascade operating rules, analyse dry-year hydrology and publish an hourly load curve.

Depends on: Data sharing by EDG and plant companies
Decision gate: Dependable-capacity study and load curve published

P3

Restore payment discipline

Adopt an arrears plan for independent producers, enforce payment of public-administration bills, scale metering and secure guarantees for new contracts.

Depends on: Budget decisions
Decision gate: Arrears plan adopted; guarantee eligibility confirmed

P4

Procure capacity technology-open, at least cost

Procure firm evening and dry-season capacity through a technology-open least-cost process, including renewables and storage where grid integration, firmness and price justify them.

Depends on: P2 sizing, P1 evacuation, P3 bankability
Decision gate: Least-cost plan and grid-integration study; transparent tender framework

P5

Decide industrial supply and regional trade

Decide how mines and refineries are supplied (grid, shared or hybrid captive) and how connection costs are shared; strengthen WAPP trade and settlement.

Depends on: P1, P2
Decision gate: Mining-supply policy; connection-cost rules

Sequence. P1 and P2 start now and in parallel; P3 runs alongside and is the precondition for private finance; P4 is sized on P2 and limited by P1 evacuation; P5 follows P1 and P2. A fixed national solar, wind, hydro or battery volume should wait for P2 and a grid-integration study. This is sequencing from the evidence, not a least-cost optimisation.

  • Hydro technical potential is 6,233 MW according to the national hydropower atlas [35]. Modelled solar-resource values are withheld from this edition pending licence confirmation. Modelled onshore wind is weak (3.8 m/s mean at 100 m) with no measurements [10]. No source demonstrates commercially viable potential for any technology.

Priorities and roles

Immediate (next 12 months)

  • Commission a Konkouré cascade dependable-capacity and operating-rules study (Souapiti, Kaléta, Garafiri, Amaria) and publish dry-season firm output.
  • Unblock financed works: Linsan–Fomi, Manéah–Linsan, the Kankan 40 MW tender and the co-financed access lots; publish a works schedule for each.
  • Adopt and implement an arrears repayment plan for independent producers and payment discipline for public-administration electricity bills.
  • Launch technology-open procurement for firm evening and dry-season capacity, sized on a published load curve.
  • Assess a frequency-containment battery: a WAPP/GIZ study recommends 13.2 MW for one hour for Guinea's share of regional primary reserve. It serves one grid service and does not address dry-season energy shortfalls.
  • Publish a daily load curve and reconcile the conflicting 2024 statistics on billed and unserved energy.

Next three years (2027–2029)

  • Complete Conakry reinforcement (Manéah–Linsan, Matoto–Manéah, northern 225 kV loop) and the Guinea–Mali and Linsan–Fomi lines.
  • Commission Amaria with its evacuation and integrate it into cascade operation.
  • Run a grid-integration and hosting-capacity study, then procure grid solar competitively, paired with storage where firmness requires it.
  • Scale prepaid and smart metering, cut technical and commercial losses, and move to a cost-reflective tariff path with social protection.
  • Deliver the GNEAP-2 mini-grids and the off-grid programme, with a mini-grid regulation and subsidy window.
  • Relocate fuel storage (Moribaya depot project) and publish delivered fuel costs.

Strategic direction to 2035

  • Select the next large hydro (Koukoutamba, Fomi, Kogbédou-Frankonédou, Grand Kinkon) on dependable output, basin rules, resettlement and financing.
  • Develop supply arrangements for mining and refinery loads (grid, shared or hybrid captive) where economic and reliable.
  • Adopt a new electricity framework law, make the regulator fully operational, publish a grid code consistent with the regional code, and define third-party access.
  • Strengthen regional trade within WAPP and secure transmission-fee settlement (OMVG, CLSG).
  • Build hydrological monitoring and climate-risk management into planning.

Method and limitations

  • Verification status: research, data extraction and drafting followed an approved research protocol and were directed and reviewed in batches by the sponsor's project owner. Extracted figures were checked against the retrieved source text within that process; they have not been independently verified by a human subject-matter expert, formally accepted or adversarially reviewed.
  • Desk research of publicly available documents, retrieved 08–15 October 2026: EDG annual reports, Government and Mission 300 compact documents, World Bank, IMF, AfDB, EIB, IsDB, EBID and WAPP documents, public datasets, and company and press reports.
  • Sources are ranked: Guinean official documents and regional bodies first, then development-finance institutions and international organisations, then data institutions and research, then company disclosures and press. Press reports are not used alone to establish a material figure; where they are the only evidence, this is stated.
  • Capacity classes (installed, available, dependable, dispatched) and energy classes (generated, delivered, billed, consumed) are kept separate. Solar capacity states MW-DC or MW-AC where the source does.
  • Project stages follow the strongest evidence: proposed, announced, active development, financing committed, under construction, operating. An announcement or memorandum never counts as a commitment. Financing amounts are labelled requested, announced, approved, signed or disbursed.
  • Wind values are model estimates, not ground measurements; modelled solar-resource values are withheld pending licence confirmation. Conflicting statistics are shown, not averaged or silently reconciled.
  • Each material figure carries a numbered reference to a public source. Inclusion in the source list does not mean the source has been independently verified; figures are as reported by their sources and have not been audited.
  • Tariffs, regulations and financing terms may have changed since the sources were published; verify with current official documents and a qualified professional before acting.
